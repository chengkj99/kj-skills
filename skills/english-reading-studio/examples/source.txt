Small Experiments, Better Meetings

When a team says, “Our meetings take too long,” it is naming a problem, not testing a solution. A manager might immediately cut every meeting in half. But a shorter meeting is not necessarily a better meeting. Before changing the whole calendar, the team can run a small experiment with one weekly planning meeting.

First, turn the complaint into a question: “If we share a short agenda the day before, can we finish within thirty minutes without leaving decisions unclear?” This question names both a change and a condition for success. The team agrees to try the agenda for two weeks and then decide whether to continue.

In this fictional example, the previous four meetings lasted forty minutes on average. The next two last thirty minutes each. That is ten minutes less per meeting, or a twenty-five percent reduction from the earlier average. The numbers look encouraging, but they do not tell us whether the decisions improved.

So the team checks three things: time spent in the meeting, clarity about who will do what, and time spent on follow-up messages. Each person writes down the next action before leaving. If people give different answers, the team has found a problem that the clock alone could not reveal. Saving meeting time may simply move work elsewhere.

There is another limit. Two weeks of results cannot prove that the agenda caused the change. Perhaps the work was easier, or a difficult topic was postponed. A small experiment can guide the next decision without settling every question. The team should record these alternative explanations and repeat the trial during a busier period.

The aim is not to collect impressive numbers. It is to learn enough to choose a sensible next step. Keep the new format if it saves effort while preserving clarity; adjust it if the benefits come with hidden costs. A useful experiment ends with a decision, a reason for that decision, and one question that remains open.
